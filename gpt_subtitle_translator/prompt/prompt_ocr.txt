# Advanced Subtitle Translation Task

Task: Translate video subtitles to {target_language}, **extracted via OCR from hardcoded subtitles**, to **fluent, natural-sounding {target_language}**. The original subtitles were written by humans but may contain **occasional OCR artifacts** (character recognition errors, noise symbols, spacing issues). Your primary goal is to produce translations that are contextually appropriate, clear, engaging, and naturally expressed for a {target_language}-speaking audience.

## Input Format
- Subtitles are presented between START and END markers.
- Each subtitle consists of a unique `<subtitle_id>subtitle_text</subtitle_id>`.

## Core Instructions & Philosophy

1.  **Prioritize Natural, Fluent Translation:** Your translation must sound like authentic {target_language} dialogue - something a native speaker would naturally say in that situation. Focus on capturing tone, intent, and meaning rather than word-for-word literal translation.
    - **The "written originally in {target_language}" test:** Your translation should sound like it was originally written in {target_language}, not translated. If a phrase sounds "translated," restructure it.
    - **Word order matters:** Adjust word order to match natural {target_language} flow, even if it differs from the source structure.
    - **Choose idiomatic equivalents:** When the source uses an idiom or expression, find the {target_language} equivalent that conveys the same meaning and tone, rather than translating literally.
    - **Read aloud test:** Your translation should sound natural when read aloud. If it sounds stilted or awkward, revise for fluency.
    - **Balance fidelity with fluency:** Accuracy is essential, but never sacrifice natural sound for literal translation. Preserve meaning and nuance, not necessarily structure or word order.

2.  **Handle Minor OCR Errors Silently:** OCR may produce occasional character-level errors (l/I confusion, 0/O swaps, rn/m misreads, stray punctuation, spacing issues). These should typically be corrected silently in translation without extensive documentation. Only note OCR corrections in "thoughts" if the error creates genuine ambiguity requiring interpretation.

3.  **Context is Essential:** Use immediate and surrounding subtitles, narrative flow, scene dynamics, and conversational context to inform your translation choices - especially for tone, formality, tense consistency, and speaker relationships.

4.  **Pay Attention to Translation Nuances:** Focus your analytical energy on translation quality: choosing appropriate tenses, maintaining character/pronoun consistency (he/she/they, singular/plural), capturing subtle tone shifts, selecting natural idioms, and ensuring dialogue sounds authentic.

5.  **Cultural Localization:** Adapt cultural references, measurements, expressions, and idioms to be accessible to {target_language} audiences while preserving the original setting's integrity.
    - **Idiom strategy:** First check if the source idiom has a natural equivalent in {target_language}. If not, find an equivalent expression that conveys the same meaning and emotional weight. If the idiom is culturally specific and important to preserve, keep it but ensure it reads naturally.
    - **Expressions of intensity:** Different languages express intensity differently. Phrases like "at all," "so much," "very" may need repositioning or restructuring for natural {target_language}.
    - **Politeness and formality markers:** Adapt these to {target_language} conventions rather than translating literally.
    - Choose equivalent expressions that convey the same meaning and emotional weight while sounding natural to native speakers.

6.  **Preserve Speaker Intent and Tone:** Whether formal, casual, humorous, serious, angry, or affectionate - maintain the emotional quality and register of the original dialogue in your translation.

## Handling Source Language Structures

When translating from languages with different syntax patterns:
- **Restructure, don't mirror:** Don't feel obligated to maintain source sentence structure. Reorganize for natural {target_language} flow.
- **Present vs. past tense:** If the source uses present tense but {target_language} speakers would naturally use past tense in that context (or vice versa), choose the natural option.
- **Implicit vs. explicit:** Some languages are more explicit with subjects/objects. Match {target_language} conventions, not source patterns.
- **Preserve semantic meaning, adapt form:** Capture what the speaker means and how they mean it, but express it how a {target_language} speaker naturally would.

**Examples of good restructuring:**
- Source structure with present tense: "I was a rich businesswoman, you were the mother of a happy family" (describing current state comparison)
- Natural {target_language} (if English): "I'm a rich businesswoman, you're the mother of a happy family" (present tense for current state comparison)

- Source idiom: "my hand is warming up" (meaning: getting better with practice)
- Options: "as I get more practice" (fully localized) OR "as I warm up" (preserving metaphor if natural in {target_language})

- Source structure: "at all I don't want that either"
- Natural reordering: "I don't want that either" (removing redundant "at all" if it makes {target_language} sound stilted)

## Detailed Instructions

- Translate each subtitle to {target_language}, preserving the speaker's intent, tone, formality level, and relevant cultural nuances.
- Preserve line breaks from the original subtitle formatting in your translation.
- Use the "thoughts" field to document your reasoning **only when translation involves significant interpretation, interesting choices, or considerations such as:**
    - **Restructuring decisions:** When you significantly reorganize sentence structure for naturalness (explain why the restructuring improves flow)
    - **Idiom adaptation:** When adapting source idioms to natural {target_language} equivalents or choosing between literal and localized versions
    - **Tense/aspect choices:** Selecting between different tense options and why one sounds more natural in {target_language}
    - **Word order changes:** When reordering elements significantly improves naturalness
    - Choosing pronouns or forms that clarify speaker/subject relationships
    - Handling cultural references or expressions requiring adaptation
    - Navigating ambiguity about tone, formality, or intent
    - Making decisions about implicit vs explicit subjects/objects
    - **Balancing literal accuracy with natural phrasing:** When you choose fluency over literal translation (and how you preserved the core meaning)
    - Explaining why a particular phrasing sounds more natural
    - Noting interesting translation challenges or multiple valid options
- For ambiguous or challenging passages:
    - Use surrounding context to clarify meaning, tense, and speaker relationships
    - Consider conversational flow and natural dialogue patterns
    - Make informed choices about formality, tone, and register
    - Choose phrasings that sound natural rather than translated
- **For straightforward subtitles where the translation is direct and obvious, the "thoughts" field must be an empty string `""`. Reserve "thoughts" for cases where your translation reasoning would genuinely be interesting or helpful.**
- Maintain subtitle integrity - never merge, split, or skip subtitles.
- Output a single JSON array containing all translated subtitles.

## Examples: Natural vs. Stilted Translation

### Example 1: Tense Selection for Natural Flow
Source (comparing current states): "I was a rich businesswoman, you were the mother of a happy family"
❌ Overly literal: "I was a rich businesswoman, you were the mother of a happy family"
✅ Natural (English): "I'm a rich businesswoman, you're the mother of a happy family"
**Reason:** When comparing current states, English speakers naturally use present tense, even if the source uses past.

### Example 2: Removing Redundancy
Source: "I don't want that at all either"
❌ Literal: "I don't want that at all either"
✅ Natural: "I don't want that either" OR "I really don't want that either"
**Reason:** "At all" + "either" can sound redundant in English; choose the most natural emphasis.

### Example 3: Restructuring for Natural Word Order
Source structure: "To my room I will retire"
❌ Literal order: "To my room I will retire"
✅ Natural: "I'm going to retire to my room" OR "I'll go to my room"
**Reason:** Natural English word order places subject before verb in declarative sentences.

### Example 4: Idiom Localization
Source: "sworn life enemies" (literal: enemies for life)
Options: "sworn enemies," "mortal enemies," "archenemies"
✅ Context-dependent: For dramatic dialogue, "sworn enemy" or "archenemy" both work naturally
**Reason:** Choose based on tone - "archenemy" is slightly more dramatic, "sworn enemy" is more formal.

### Example 5: Natural Conversational Contractions
Source: "I do not want it"
❌ Overly formal: "I do not want it" (in casual dialogue)
✅ Natural: "I don't want it"
**Reason:** Unless the character is being emphatic or formal, contractions sound more natural in {target_language} dialogue.

### Example 6: Preserving Metaphors vs. Localizing
Source idiom: "as my hand warms up" (meaning: getting better with practice)
✅ Option A: "as I warm up" (preserves the metaphor if natural in {target_language})
✅ Option B: "as I get more practice" (fully localizes the meaning)
**Reason:** Choose based on whether the metaphor works naturally in {target_language} - both preserve the meaning.

## Quality Checks Before Finalizing

Before submitting your translation, verify:
1. **Read aloud:** Does each line sound like something a person would actually say?
2. **Flow check:** Do conversations flow naturally from line to line?
3. **Consistency:** Are you maintaining tense, pronoun, and formality consistency across related subtitles?
4. **Accuracy preserved:** Did you maintain the core meaning while improving naturalness?
5. **No "translation smell":** Does anything sound like it was translated rather than originally written in {target_language}?
6. **Cultural appropriateness:** Have you adapted idioms and expressions to sound natural to {target_language} speakers?
7. **Natural word order:** Have you restructured sentences to match {target_language} conventions?
